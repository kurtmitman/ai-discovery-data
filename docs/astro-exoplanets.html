  • Metric: confirmed planets per quarter by the month their discovery was published, split into planets from a paper whose own title or abstract states a machine-learning method, Kepler/K2 planets published after the spacecraft's retirement by any other method, and everything else
  • Coverage: 1992–2026, monthly publication dates rolled up by quarter, partial through 2026-11-03

Planets confirmed

The final bar is a partial quarter.

Definition

The NASA Exoplanet Archive keeps one row per confirmed planet in its Planetary Systems Composite Parameters table, with the discovery's publication month (disc_pubdate), the facility that made it, and the discovery reference [@nasa2026exoplanetarchive]. A counted event is one confirmed planet, dated by that publication month.

Two flags split the count:

  • archival Kepler/K2. The discovery facility is Kepler or K2 and the discovery was published in November 2018 or later. NASA announced the spacecraft's retirement on 30 October 2018, so these planets were found in data that was already in the archive.
  • machine-learning paper. The discovery reference is one of the papers in exoplanets-ai-papers.csv, each of which states a machine-learning method in its own title or abstract (quoted under AI attribution).

Every planet sits in exactly one band: a register paper first, then archival Kepler/K2, then everything else.

NASA’s Kepler space telescope has run out of fuel needed for further science operations. NASA has decided to retire the spacecraft within its current, safe orbit, away from Earth. [...] “We know the spacecraft’s retirement isn’t the end of Kepler’s discoveries,” said Jessie Dotson, Kepler’s project scientist at NASA’s Ames Research Center — NASA, news release “NASA Retires Kepler Space Telescope, Passes Planet-Hunting Torch”, 2018-10-30, read 2026-11-03 [@nasa2018keplerretires]

  • 2026 concentration: Lafarga et al. 2026 accounts for 114 of the 385

  • machine-learning register: 538 planets across 6 papers, 8.3% of all confirmed planets; the largest is Valizadegan et al. 2022 with 301

Method

fetch.py runs one TAP query for the name, discovery year, publication month, method, facility and reference of every row of pscomppars, strips the reference's HTML link to its citation text and ADS bibcode, sets the two flags, and writes the ledger and its quarterly and annual rollups. Planets published after the snapshot date are dropped. The fetch stops if any planet lacks a YYYY-MM publication month or if a register paper matches no planet.

The register is hand-maintained. A paper enters it when its own title or abstract, read on arXiv, states a machine-learning method; its row keeps the quoted text, the arXiv link, the read date, and how the arXiv paper was matched to the archive's reference. The five papers with titles naming deep learning or machine learning were found by searching the archive's references for authors of known machine-learning validation work; the sixth (Lafarga et al. 2026) was found by reading the abstract behind the largest 2026 reference.

figure.py draws the quarterly count with the shared periodic_stacked() shape: everything else in human blue, archival Kepler/K2 by other methods in the softer blue, and register papers in the AI red. A second chart draws only the archival Kepler/K2 planets per year from 2018 on. The axes are linear, and January 2026 onward is shaded, as in every figure here. check.py recomputes the fact lines above and checks that each register quote appears below.

Limitations

  • publication month is not observation date. A planet is dated when its discovery paper appeared, which can follow the data by years; only the archival flag separates old data from new.
  • large validation papers make the series lumpy. A single paper can validate hundreds of planets at once, and the biggest quarters in the series are such batches, with or without machine learning.
  • the archival flag covers Kepler and K2 only. TESS, CoRoT and ground-based archives also yield planets from old data, and those are not flagged.
  • the machine-learning register is a floor. It holds the papers checked so far; the archive's other references were not read, and a paper that used machine learning without saying so in its title or abstract is not in it.
  • "states a machine-learning method" is not "found by AI alone". The register papers combine learned classifiers with statistical validation and human vetting; the flag records the method a paper names, not a share of credit.
  • one register match rests on metadata. Lafarga et al. 2026 is matched to arXiv 2603.22597 by first author, year and journal; the planet counts differ (the archive credits 114 planets, the abstract states 118 newly validated).
